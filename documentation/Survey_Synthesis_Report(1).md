**SURVEY SYNTHESIS REPORT**

*Minibus Fare Collection & Passenger Management Study*

Routes Covered: Dalipuga (DATRANSCO) • Buru-un (DONSALS)

*Total Respondents: 20 Conductors (Konduktors)*

# **I. Overview**

This synthesis consolidates the findings from a structured survey administered to twenty (20) minibus conductors across two routes in the Iligan City area: Dalipuga under DATRANSCO (n=10) and Buru-un under DONSALS (n=10). The survey instrument was composed of four parts: current daily operations, daily struggles measured through a 4-point Likert scale, acceptance of a proposed new ticketing system, and open-ended interview questions. The purpose of the study is to establish the need for a digitized, card-based fare collection system that operates independently of internet connectivity.

# **II. Current Daily Operations**

## **A. Passenger Counting Method**

Both routes show a strong reliance on manual counting as the primary method of tracking passenger load.

| Method | Dalipuga (DATRANSCO) | Buru-un (DONSALS) | Combined Total |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Manual counting (eyes/memory) | 8 out of 10 | 8 out of 10 | 16 out of 20 (80%) |
| Based on tickets issued | 1 out of 10 | 2 out of 10 | 3 out of 20 (15%) |
| Other (headcount/by seating) | 1 out of 10 | 0 out of 10 | 1 out of 20 (5%) |

**Key Finding:** An overwhelming 80% of conductors across both routes rely purely on memory or visual estimation to count passengers. This method is inherently error-prone, particularly during peak hours when buses are crowded, and represents a significant operational vulnerability for fare reconciliation.

## **B. Ticketing Method**

| Method | Dalipuga (DATRANSCO) | Buru-un (DONSALS) | Combined Total |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Printed paper tickets | 5 out of 10 | 9 out of 10 | 14 out of 20 (70%) |
| Traditional (punch/machine) tickets | 5 out of 10 | 1 out of 10 | 6 out of 20 (30%) |

**Key Finding:** Printed paper tickets dominate across both routes, especially in the Buru-un route where 90% still rely on them. Notably, an additional verbal account from a driver in the Buru-un (DONSALS) route reveals that a designated office staff member visits each bus every 6:00 PM to physically collect all used tickets and cash, and then manually reconciles whether these figures match. This procedure confirms that ticket-based verification is still entirely manual, conducted at the end of the shift, and is highly susceptible to discrepancies from misplaced, lost, or uncollected tickets.

# **IV. Acceptance of the Proposed System**

Two acceptance questions were posed to respondents in Part 3:

| Acceptance Question | Yes | No |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Would you use a handheld device that auto-calculates fare? | 20 out of 20 (100%) | 0 |
| Would you use reusable tap-and-return plastic cards instead of paper tickets? | 18 out of 20 (90%) | 2 out of 20 (10%) |

**Key Finding:** All 20 conductors expressed willingness to adopt an automatic fare calculation device, reflecting strong openness to technological intervention. Acceptance of the reusable card system was nearly universal at 90%, with only two respondents declining. One of the dissenting respondents from Buru-un noted that paper tickets are currently easier and less complicated. This suggests that onboarding and system design must prioritize simplicity and minimal learning curve.

# **V. Interview Synthesis — Key Themes from Conductor Responses**

## **Theme 1: Change-Making and Cash Management is the Primary Stressor**

Across both routes, the most frequently cited hardship was giving the correct change to passengers, especially when passengers tender large bills. Several conductors mentioned being blamed for double-changing passengers or forgetting to return change to specific individuals due to the volume of transactions during peak hours. Representative responses included accounts of passengers complaining about incorrect change, conductors forgetting who was still owed change, and the stress of managing multiple fare transactions simultaneously during standing-room-only conditions.

## **Theme 2: Disputed Payments and Passenger Dishonesty**

Multiple conductors from both routes reported incidents where passengers denied receiving tickets or claimed they had not yet been issued one despite having been served. In some cases, passengers claimed not to have received change even after payment was completed. These incidents occurred approximately two to three times a week for several conductors, and more frequently during rush hours. The absence of a verifiable electronic record makes it impossible to resolve these disputes objectively.

## **Theme 3: Persistent Dead Zones Along Both Routes**

Conductors consistently identified specific geographic segments of their routes as areas with poor or no mobile signal. For the Dalipuga (DATRANSCO) route, the areas of Tag-ibo, Sta. Fe, and Barinaut were repeatedly cited. For the Buru-un (DONSALS) route, the Timoga area — particularly near forested hillside zones — was universally identified as a signal dead zone. This directly informs the system design requirement that the proposed device must function in a fully offline capacity.

## **Theme 4: Strong Reception for the Proposed Card-Based System**

When asked about a small handheld device with tap-and-calculate functionality, the majority of conductors expressed enthusiasm. Common reasoning included: faster transactions, reduced stress from fare computation, elimination of the need for exact change, and greater accuracy in end-of-day reconciliation. Several conductors recognized that the system could also reduce passenger disputes since the fare would be objectively computed and displayed. One conductor from Dalipuga did note that training or a seminar would be necessary for adoption, which points to the need for a comprehensive onboarding plan.

# **VI. Additional Finding: End-of-Day Ticket-Cash Reconciliation (Buru-un — DONSALS)**

A verbal account provided by a driver from the Buru-un (DONSALS) route reveals a noteworthy operational procedure: an office staff member is deployed to each minibus unit at approximately 6:00 PM every evening to collect all used (punchable/perforated) tickets and the day’s cash collection. The staff member then attempts to reconcile whether the number of tickets issued matches the total cash collected.

This finding is particularly significant for the thesis for several reasons:

* The reconciliation process is entirely manual and reactive, meaning errors are only discovered at the end of the day when corrections are no longer possible.

* The use of punchable tickets provides a physical count of boarded passengers, but this count can be undermined by lost tickets, incorrectly punched tickets, or deliberate misuse.

* The system creates a daily administrative burden for office personnel who must physically travel to each bus unit.

* A digital fare collection system would render this end-of-day visit unnecessary by automatically generating real-time transaction logs accessible to the operator.

# **VII. Identified Problems for the Thesis**

Based on the totality of survey findings, the following core problems are identified and must be addressed by the proposed system:

| Problem | Evidence | Impact |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Inaccurate passenger counting | 80% of conductors rely solely on memory or visual estimation | Unreliable ridership data; basis for revenue reconciliation is weak |
| Fare and change computation errors | 85%–90% agree that managing exact change and fare tracking is a daily struggle | Revenue leakage; conductor stress; passenger dissatisfaction |
| Cash-to-passenger count mismatch | 80% confirm daily discrepancy between cash collected and passenger count | Unverifiable revenue loss; potential for dishonesty |
| Disputed transactions with no audit trail | Conductor interviews report 2–3 disputes per week on average | No objective resolution possible; erodes trust |
| Environmental waste from paper tickets | 90% agree paper tickets produce litter | Environmental concern; operational inefficiency |
| Offline-only operational environment | 80% of conductors report dead zones; named areas: Tag-ibo, Sta. Fe, Barinaut, Timoga | Internet-dependent solutions are not viable for this deployment context |
| Manual end-of-day reconciliation | DONSALS driver account: office staff collect tickets and cash every 6 PM | Administrative overhead; delayed error detection; scalability constraint |

# **VIII. Implications for System Design**

The survey findings collectively inform the following non-negotiable requirements for the proposed smart fare collection system:

* Fully offline functionality — the device must operate without requiring any cellular or internet connection, with optional sync when connectivity becomes available.

* Automatic fare computation — the device must calculate fares instantly upon card tap, eliminating the need for manual computation by the conductor.

* Reusable card-based ticketing — thick plastic tap-and-return cards must replace single-use paper tickets to reduce waste and enable transaction recording.

* Digital transaction logging — each tap event must be stored locally and linked to fare data, enabling automatic reconciliation without end-of-day manual ticket collection.

* Simple and trainable interface — system adoption requires a minimal learning curve given that some conductors expressed the need for orientation before use.

* Passenger dispute resolution capability — transaction records must be retrievable to resolve contested payments objectively.

# **IX. Conclusion**

The survey results from both the Dalipuga (DATRANSCO) and Buru-un (DONSALS) routes establish a clear, quantifiable, and urgent need for a digitized fare collection system. Manual processes currently dominate every aspect of the conductor’s workflow — from passenger counting and fare computation to ticket issuance and end-of-day reconciliation — and each of these manual processes generates measurable inefficiency, error, and stress.

Most importantly, all 20 respondents expressed willingness to adopt an automatic fare calculation device, and 90% were open to reusable card-based tickets. The high acceptance rate, combined with the severity and frequency of identified problems, strongly justifies the development and deployment of the proposed system. The additional insight from the DONSALS driver further reinforces the systemic and administrative burden that currently exists, which a digital system would directly resolve.
